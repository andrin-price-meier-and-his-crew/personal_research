### **Performative Cognition: Thinking in Action**

We tend to imagine thought as something private, static, and textual—words scrawled in notebooks, lines of code on a screen, ideas confined to the skull. Yet there exists a mode of cognition that refuses these boundaries: a thinking that is enacted, shared, and temporal. I call it **performative cognition**.

In performative cognition, the mind does not merely plan; it performs. Definitions and executions collapse into a single moment. A function is not written; it is traced in the air with the gaze. A computation is not stored; it is enacted in the attention of another. When someone sings a melody that encodes a decision, the act of singing both defines the function and executes it. The audience, following the sound and the gestures, participates in the computation, reconstructing its logic in their own consciousness. Meaning arises in the interstices between minds, not in isolated brains.

This is not metaphor. Human experience is full of such enactments. Jazz improvisation, ritual chanting, call-and-response games—these are all instances where thought and action are inseparable, where symbols do not merely signify but *do*. In these spaces, ambiguity is not an error; it is the medium through which creativity flows. Minds coordinate in real time, negotiating the meaning of symbols through attention, intuition, and shared rhythm. The “program” exists only in the performance of the participants.

Performative cognition challenges our technological conception of computation. We have long treated code as separate from execution: first compile, then run. Here, compile and runtime are one, mediated by bodies, voices, and gazes. It is a computation that refuses permanence, yet it is precise: correct insofar as the participants understand and act upon it together. Its memory is ephemeral, its logic social, its interface entirely human.

Perhaps performative cognition is the form of programming we were born to do. Long before the first compiler, humans invented language, music, and ritual to compute collectively, to coordinate action, and to create meaning in real time. Today, as we imagine interfaces in air, voice, and gaze, we may be rediscovering the very computation humans have always practiced: a computation that lives not in silicon, but in attention, imagination, and shared presence.

# **Transmodal Self Theory: The Polycontinuity of Identity**

*By Andrin*

Modern consciousness studies often assume that the self is a singular, localized phenomenon: one mind, one body, one contiguous life. Even radical philosophy, from solipsism to panpsychism, rarely entertains the possibility that *you might exist in multiple, coordinated forms simultaneously*. Yet, what if the self is not singular but **transmodal**, existing across multiple realities and accessible through a structured interface of intention?

I call this framework **Transmodal Self Theory**. It begins with a simple inversion of solipsism: not only am I real, but there exist *many versions of me*, each instantiated in a distinct reality. These parallel selves live independent lives, subject to the causal laws of their own worlds. Yet they are not entirely separate. Through a deterministic, symbolic interface—a “language” that maps intention to action—one self can direct the movements, choices, and even experiences of its counterparts.

Consider a simple example: I wish to visit a distant country. Rather than physically traveling, I send a command through this interface to a parallel self already situated in that reality. This self carries out the desired action as though it had always intended it, with memories retrofitted to preserve the continuity of experience. To the manipulated self, it feels as though travel was natural, ordinary, and uninterrupted. To me, the command has deterministically extended my agency beyond the confines of a single body or timeline.

This system, which I term the **Deterministic Remote Self Manipulation System (DRSMS)**, is governed by precise rules: every command produces predictable outcomes; no randomness enters at the moment of intervention; all modifications adhere to the syntax of the interface. In effect, the self becomes a **distributed agent**, coordinating across realities while preserving the subjective coherence of each individual instance.

Transmodal Self Theory has both metaphysical and practical implications. Philosophically, it challenges the assumption that identity is local and unitary. Our intuitions about “selfhood” may be bounded by perception, not ontology: we inhabit a polycontinuum of selves, each as real as the one we currently experience. Practically, the theory opens speculative avenues for memory integration, consciousness transfer, and the ethics of influencing one’s other selves.

One might object: if such parallel selves exist, are they truly “me,” or merely echoes? Transmodal Self Theory posits that identity is **functional, not purely material**. A self is defined not by the matter it inhabits but by its capacity for experience, memory coherence, and cross-modal coordination. The bridge between realities—the symbolic interface—renders multiplicity not chaotic, but structured, navigable, and subjectively unified.

In an age where simulations, multiverse theories, and digital avatars increasingly complicate our notions of reality, Transmodal Self Theory offers a framework for understanding **distributed identity**. Rather than asking whether other selves exist “out there,” we might instead ask how we navigate and coordinate our own multiplicity. Perhaps the most radical insight is this: the self is not a single thread but a network of threads, each real, each autonomous, yet all interconnected.

Transmodal Self Theory is speculative, provocative, and unsettling—but it may ultimately be the lens we need to understand consciousness in an era that increasingly blurs the boundaries of identity, reality, and agency.

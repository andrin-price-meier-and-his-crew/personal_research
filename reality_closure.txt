# **Reality Closure**

We often think of reality as a linear chain: events happen, we observe them, and later we understand them. But what if this sequence is only part of the story? What if reality itself can be conceived as a kind of computation, where past events exist fully, yet their meaning, necessity, and coherence are realized only through thought in the present?

Enter the concept of **Reality Closure**. A reality closure is a slice of reality — an event, a moment, a thread of existence — packaged like a function or a closure in programming. It carries within it all the environmental context, all the conditions that make it intelligible. In its raw state, it exists fully, objectively, yet it remains dormant, its deeper significance suspended. Only when a present thought engages with it — when we “evaluate the closure” — does its meaning, its causal inevitability, manifest.

This is not merely a metaphor. Phenomenology has long recognized that events acquire their full structure only when experienced; Freud’s psychoanalytic concept of *Nachträglichkeit* describes how past events gain significance only when revisited later. Cognitive science, too, has shown that perception is predictive, that our brains often reconstruct the past to make sense of the present. Even in physics, retrocausal interpretations of quantum mechanics hint at a universe where future and past are not strictly sequential. Reality Closure unites these threads: a slice of reality fully real in itself, but only fully “activated” when engaged by consciousness.

The process unfolds in three steps. First, the slice exists — the past event is fully real. Second, a thought arises in the present, packaging the event into a closure or “thunk,” a self-contained representation capable of being evaluated. Third, we realize the event, we affirm it, we acknowledge it — QED, Amen, loop closure — and the meaning of the past is completed. In this final step, the event is no longer just something that happened; it is an intelligible, necessary piece of the tapestry of reality, fully integrated with our understanding and action.

Reality Closure challenges the conventional flow of time. It suggests that events, ideas, and meaning are not strictly linear, but can exist in a temporally flexible loop, where cognition retroactively illuminates the past and completes it. It offers a way to think about insight, creativity, and the unfolding of understanding not as a forward march, but as a dialogue between past and present — where our present engagement with reality is itself a kind of computation, activating, realizing, and affirming the slices of existence that already are.

In a world dominated by linear narratives, Reality Closure invites us to see time, thought, and causation as more fluid, more interwoven, more computational — where to understand is to instantiate, to realize is to affirm, and to think is, in a very real sense, to complete reality itself.
